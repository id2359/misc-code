Condition numbers κ(A) = σ₁ / σ₂

A matrix maps the unit circle to an ellipse. The condition number is the ratio of the most stretching (σ₁, long axis) to the least stretching (σ₂, short axis) — it measures how unevenly the transformation distorts space, and how much errors can be amplified when you solve Ax = b.

What κ measures

Any 2×2 matrix rotates, stretches along two perpendicular directions (by σ₁ and σ₂), then rotates again — that's the SVD. The unit circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axes σ₁ and σ₂. κ = σ₁/σ₂ is the ellipse's aspect ratio:

  • κ = 1 — circle stays a circle (rotations, uniform scaling). Perfectly conditioned.
  • κ large — a squashed sliver. Nearly loses a dimension.
  • κ = ∞ — the ellipse collapses to a line: A is singular, no inverse.

Why errors blow up

Solving Ax = b means applying A⁻¹, which stretches by 1/σ₂ in the direction A squashed most. An error δb pointing that way gets magnified by 1/σ₂, while b itself may only shrink by 1/σ₁ — the relative error grows by up to κ.

Rule of thumb: you lose about log₁₀ κ significant digits. With 16-digit floating point, κ ≈ 10¹⁰ leaves only ~6 trustworthy digits.

Where it shows up

  • Linear systems — Gaussian elimination on ill-conditioned A gives garbage even with exact arithmetic on rounded inputs.
  • Least squares / regression — nearly collinear features ⇒ huge κ ⇒ wildly unstable coefficients. Normal equations square it: κ(AᵀA) = κ(A)².
  • Optimization / ML — gradient descent converges slowly on ill-conditioned loss surfaces; that's why we normalize features and precondition.
  • Graphics & geometry — inverting a nearly-degenerate transform (thin triangles, near-parallel view rays) is numerically fragile.
